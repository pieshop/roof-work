# Roof / Chimney / Gable Repair – Cost Estimate

## Working Budget

Based on the current scope of works, I would budget approximately:

**£12,000–£18,000 including VAT**

A quote outside this range is possible, particularly if more extensive structural masonry or roof repairs become apparent once scaffolding is erected and the affected areas can be properly inspected.

## Roof Repair vs Replacement

The **£12,000–£18,000** working budget assumes that the existing slate roof is substantially retained and **repaired/overhauled rather than completely stripped and re-covered**.

The Level 2 survey describes the house as constructed circa 1900. The roof is believed to be the **original slate roof**.

There is **no roofing underlay beneath the existing slates**. This is consistent with the age and original construction of the roof and is not by itself evidence that the roof has failed.

The age of the roof does not automatically mean that complete replacement is necessary, particularly if the original natural slates remain sound. However, the condition of the slate fixings, battens and roof timbers needs to be assessed carefully.

A key purpose of the contractor inspections is therefore to establish whether:

1. The original roof remains fundamentally sound and is worth repairing/overhauling;
2. The original slates remain good but the fixings/battens are sufficiently deteriorated to justify stripping and re-slating with the original slates; or
3. There is sufficiently widespread deterioration that a more complete reroof using replacement slates would be more economical.

The fact that scaffolding will already be required for the chimney and structural work should be taken into account when comparing these options.

## Option 1 – Repair / Overhaul Existing Roof

This is the option assumed by the current **£12,000–£18,000** overall project budget.

The existing roof would remain substantially undisturbed.

The repair/overhaul allowance includes:

- Refixing/replacing slipped, broken or defective slates
- Localised repairs to battens and roof timbers where accessible and necessary
- Ridge, junction, abutment and flashing repairs
- Making good the roof following chimney reduction/removal
- Addressing areas where daylight or water ingress is evident

It does **not** include stripping the complete roof or installing roofing underlay beneath the existing slates.

During contractor inspections, ask:

> In your opinion, is the existing slate roof worth overhauling, or is its overall condition such that I should consider stripping and re-slating while the scaffolding is already in place?

Given the age of the roof, also ask:

> If the existing roof is overhauled rather than stripped and re-slated, what sort of further service life would you realistically expect it to have?

The expected remaining life is important. A repair that provides a worthwhile further period of service may make good economic sense. An overhaul followed by the need to strip the roof only a few years later would be much less attractive.

## Option 2 – Strip and Re-slate Using Existing Slates

There is currently no roofing underlay beneath the original slates.

If the original slates remain in good condition but their fixings and/or battens are deteriorating, an intermediate option between local repair and replacement with a completely new roof should be considered.

The likely process would be:

1. Carefully strip the existing slates
2. Sort and retain sound reusable slates
3. Inspect the exposed rafters and other roof timbers
4. Carry out any necessary timber repairs
5. Install a modern breathable roofing underlay
6. Install new treated battens
7. Re-slate using the retained original slates
8. Use suitable matching/reclaimed slates to replace damaged or unusable originals
9. Renew associated ridge, flashing and junction details as necessary

This would retain as much of the original slate as practical while renewing the underlying roof system and introducing a modern roofing underlay.

It may be particularly attractive if the original slates themselves have substantial remaining life but the fixings or battens are reaching the end of theirs.

Some slate loss should be expected during stripping, so it is unlikely that 100% of the original slates could be reused.

Ask contractors:

> If the original slates are still good, would it make sense to strip and re-slate the roof using the existing slates, with new membrane and battens underneath?

If this option is recommended, establish:

- What proportion of the existing slates they expect to salvage
- Whether matching reclaimed slates are readily available for any shortfall
- Whether all battens would be replaced
- What breathable roofing membrane they propose
- What timber repairs they anticipate
- Whether ventilation or insulation changes would be required
- How its cost compares with both localised overhaul and reroofing with replacement slate
- What guarantee would apply to a roof re-covered predominantly with the original slates

## Option 3 – Complete Reroof Using Replacement Slates

Given the age of the original roof, complete reroofing should be considered as a realistic alternative scenario, but **not assumed to be necessary**.

The decision should be based on the actual condition of the roof rather than its age alone.

Contractors should assess:

- Condition and remaining life of the original slates
- Evidence of widespread nail sickness or fixing failure
- Condition of battens
- Absence of roofing underlay beneath the existing roof
- Condition of rafters, purlins and other roof timbers
- Extent of previous patch repairs
- Number and distribution of slipped or defective slates
- Condition of ridge, valleys, abutments and flashings
- Evidence of historic or current water ingress
- Whether a repair/overhaul is likely to provide a worthwhile further service life
- Whether sufficient original slate could be salvaged to make re-slating with the existing material practical

If the original slates themselves are significantly deteriorated or the salvage rate would be too low, complete reroofing with replacement slate may be more appropriate.

A complete reroof would be **additional to the current repair-based budget**.

The additional cost will depend heavily on:

- Total roof area
- Condition and salvageability of the existing slates
- Type and cost of replacement slate
- New roofing underlay
- New battens
- Extent of timber repairs
- Ridge, valley, abutment and flashing details
- Insulation and ventilation requirements
- Building Regulations requirements
- How much of the scaffolding required for the existing project can also be used for the reroof

There is currently no firm budget estimate for this option. A separate current-market estimate should be prepared if contractor inspections indicate that a complete reroof is warranted.

## Comparing the Three Roof Options

If contractors identify significant age-related deterioration, ideally obtain comparable prices for:

1. **Repair/overhaul of the existing roof**
2. **Strip and re-slate using as much of the original slate as possible, with new underlay and battens**
3. **Complete reroof using replacement slate**

The decision should not be based solely on the cheapest immediate price.

Also consider:

- Expected remaining life of each option
- Quality and remaining life of the original slate
- Cost of future scaffolding if reroofing is postponed
- Amount of original material retained
- Improvement provided by adding modern roofing underlay
- Condition of battens and roof timbers
- Guarantees offered
- Likelihood of further significant repairs being required

## Main Cost Risks

### Structural work

One of the biggest uncertainties is probably **not the chimney removal**.

The area most likely to materially affect the final price is the combination of:

- Bulging high-level gable/flank masonry
- Defective or missing masonry around the purlin bearing
- Additional wall ties
- Reinstatement of proper support to the roof purlin

If these require relatively localised repairs, the overall project should remain towards the expected range.

If a substantial section of the high-level wall needs to be taken down and rebuilt, costs could increase significantly.

### Original roof

The second major uncertainty is the condition of the original slate roof on a circa-1900 house.

The original roof has **no roofing underlay beneath the slates**. This is not by itself evidence that the roof has failed, but any complete strip and re-slate would provide an opportunity to install a modern breathable roofing underlay as part of the renewed roof build-up.

If the original slates, fixings, battens and supporting roof construction remain sufficiently sound for a worthwhile overhaul, the existing repair-based budget remains appropriate.

If the slates remain good but inspection identifies widespread fixing failure or deteriorated battens, stripping and re-slating using the original slates may be a sensible intermediate option.

If inspection identifies extensive slate deterioration as well as problems with fixings, battens or the underlying structure, reroofing using replacement slate may become appropriate.

This is why independent opinions from several contractors will be particularly useful before deciding which approach to take.

## Potential Cost Advantage of a Single Package

Carrying out all of the high-level work together should provide an important economy.

The same scaffolding can provide access for:

- Chimney reduction/removal
- Gable/flank wall repairs
- Wall ties
- Purlin support work
- Slate repairs
- Ridge and flashing repairs
- Guttering/downpipes
- Stripping and re-slating, if ultimately considered necessary

This avoids paying for separate scaffolding on several future repair jobs.

The presence of scaffolding also changes the economics of the roof decision.

If the original roof is likely to require stripping and re-slating relatively soon, undertaking that work while suitable scaffolding is already present may be more economical than carrying out an overhaul now and paying for another scaffold several years later.

Conversely, the existence of scaffolding is **not in itself a reason to strip an otherwise serviceable original slate roof**.
